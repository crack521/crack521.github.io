REND Mitigating the Measurement Problem for Cost-Constrained Network Dismantling with Deep Reinforcement Learning

1Abstract

Network dismantling, the removal of a minimal set of nodes to fragment a network, underpins critical applications from epidemic control to infrastructure protection. Despite extensive research, only a few studies have tackled the heterogeneity of removal costs. However, here we reveal that even these remain entangled in a fundamental measurement problem: the metric can be gamed by removing massive numbers of low-cost nodes, which is optimal in theory but impractical in practice due to hidden overheads. To mitigate this problem, we propose RGCC, a corrected metric to penalize removal scale and reward true fragmentation. Using RGCC, we develop REND, a deep reinforcement learning model that, under equal cost, removes half the nodes of the best baseline for more than twice the decline in giant connected component (GCC) and six times more fragments. Interpretability inspires a simple heuristic, Boundary-Cut, that matches REND's performance without training. Our work redefines cost-constrained network dismantling and opens up new directions for understanding network resilience and cost-effective system design. More broadly, it demonstrates how deep reinforcement learning can expose hidden flaws in evaluation metrics and serve as a discovery tool for simple, transferable heuristics in complex optimization problems.

2The Measurement Problem

Cost-constrained dismantling looks for a node-removal sequence \((v_1,\dots,v_N)\) that minimizes the Accumulated Normalized Connectivity (ANC) score under a connectivity measure \(\sigma\),

\[ R_\sigma(v_1,\dots,v_N)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{\sigma(\mathcal G_k)}{\sigma(\mathcal G)}\,c(v_k) \]
which conventionally takes \(\sigma=\sgcc\), giving
\[ \Rgcc=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{\sgcc(\mathcal G_k)}{N}\,c(v_k) \]

where \(\sgcc\) is the giant-connected-component (GCC) size and \(c(v_k)\) the normalized removal cost, with \(\sum_k c(v_k)=1\). A lower \(\Rgcc\) curve is conventionally read as a more cost-effective strategy. The catch: a drop in GCC can come from two very different processes — genuine structural fragmentation (removing critical nodes that disconnect large portions of the network) or trivial shrinkage (removing many cheap leaves so that fewer nodes simply remain). The metric rewards both equally, and because peripheral nodes are nearly free, mass-harvesting them is the cheapest way to drive the curve down.

FINDER — a deep-RL agent trained on millions of graphs to minimize exactly this objective — learned precisely that shortcut. On the 16-node example of Fig. 1, both strategies are run until the residual GCC reaches 6: FINDER gets there at a lower total cost (10 vs. 14) but pays for it with 10 node removals instead of 2. On the HI-II-14 protein network, a deliberately trivial MinCost greedy baseline — always delete the cheapest remaining node — matches or even beats the highly optimized agent under \(\Rgcc\). When a structure-blind heuristic ties a model trained on millions of graphs, the problem is the metric, not the model.

Why is such a "solution" not optimal in practice? First, operational infeasibility: cost reflects the difficulty of a single operation, but the number of removals determines how many operations must be executed, each consuming manpower, intelligence, logistics and risk. Fifty simple operations can be harder than ten complex ones. Second, structural fragility: mass-removing leaves exploits topological redundancy rather than genuine vulnerability, and the damage is easily undone by adding a few edges or nodes.

3The Remedy: the RGCC Metric

We replace the static denominator \(N\) with a dynamic one — the number of remaining nodes \(\Nleft = N-k\):

\[ \mathrm{RGCC}_k=\frac{\sgcc(\mathcal G_k)}{\Nleft}=\frac{\sgcc(\mathcal G_k)}{N-k} \]

Since \(\sigma_{\rm rgcc}(\mathcal G)=\sgcc(\mathcal G)/N=1\), the evaluation objective becomes

\[ \Rrgcc(v_1,\dots,v_N)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{\sigma_{\rm rgcc}(\mathcal G_k)}{\sigma_{\rm rgcc}(\mathcal G)}\,c(v_k) =\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{\sgcc(\mathcal G_k)}{N-k}\,c(v_k) \]

One character changes — the static denominator \(N\) becomes the dynamic \(N-k\) — and the scale of removal enters the objective directly. RGCC keeps the same computational complexity as \(\sgcc\), so it remains usable on networks with millions of nodes, and it preserves strategy rankings in the uniform-cost setting.

Why this simple change works — three effects, each shown live:

(i) Penalizes ineffective removals removing a node outside the GCC shrinks \(\Nleft\) while \(\sgcc\) stays put, so RGCC rises.
(ii) Distinguishes fragmentation from shrinkage a single leaf removal leaves RGCC unchanged, while a removal that disconnects many nodes drops it sharply.
(iii) Encodes a cost–scale trade-off every removal shrinks the denominator \(\Nleft\), raising the bar for all future actions.
Effect (ii) is exact: removing a node inside the GCC lowers RGCC if and only if the number of nodes that leave the GCC, \(\Delta=\sgcc(\mathcal G_k)-\sgcc(\mathcal G_{k+1})\), exceeds \(\sgcc(\mathcal G_k)/\Nleft\). While the network is still connected that threshold is exactly 1, so deleting a single leaf (\(\Delta=1\)) buys nothing — only a removal that disconnects at least two nodes helps. A node whose removal triggers cascading disconnection gives \(\Delta\gg1\) and a steep drop. RGCC thus rewards structural collapse, not scale reduction.

4REND: an RGCC-Optimized RL Agent

Following the FINDER paradigm, REND is trained fully data-driven on small synthetic Barabási–Albert graphs (30–50 nodes), with no hand-designed heuristics. Each dismantling episode is a Markov decision process: the state is the residual graph, the action selects a node to remove, and the reward is the decrease in the RGCC-based objective; the episode ends once the network is completely disconnected. A graph-neural-network encoder embeds nodes (initialized with degree/cost features) via neighborhood aggregation; a parameterized Q-function decoder scores actions. Training runs for \(10^6\) episodes over roughly \(2\times10^5\) distinct topologies, with \(n\)-step returns, a \(5\times10^4\)-transition replay buffer and \(\epsilon\) annealed from 1.0 to 0.05 over the first \(10^4\) episodes.

Directly optimizing the RGCC reward is unstable because the time-varying denominator \((N-k)\) couples three node-dependent terms. We therefore train on a lower bound that fixes the denominator at \(N\) and decouples connectivity from cost:

\[ R \ge R_{\rm lb} = -\sum_{k=1}^{N_\tau}\Big[\frac{\sgcc(\mathcal G_k)}{N}+c(v_k)\Big] \]

5Performance

REND is trained once on 30–50 node BA graphs and evaluated, unchanged, on larger synthetic graphs and nine real-world networks spanning criminal, biological, communication, infrastructure and social domains — from thousands to millions of nodes. Baselines cover node centrality (HDA, CI), optimal decycling (MinSum, BPD, CoreHD), learned dismantling (GDM, FINDER) and cost-aware partitioning (GND). Under the corrected \(\Rrgcc\) metric REND consistently and significantly outperforms all of them — for both degree- and random-cost, on synthetic and real data. Compared with the second-best method, REND lowers \(\Rrgcc\) by an average of 4.0% (vs. MinSum) on synthetic graphs and 20.7% (vs. GND) on real networks. Notably, FINDER — highly optimized for the old metric — now performs the worst under RGCC.

GND is the informative counter-example. Because it was designed to cut a small set of structurally critical nodes rather than to exploit a metric loophole, it performs strongly under both the GCC and the RGCC metric, consistently ranking second here. That points to the broader lesson of the paper: algorithm design should focus on solving the problem itself, not on catering to any specific evaluation metric. A strategy built on that principle may not top every possible metric, but it will never collapse when the metric changes.

6More Fragments, Fewer Nodes

Compared with GND on the DBLP citation network (12,495 nodes), at the same 60% cost budget both reduce the GCC to ~10% of its original size — but REND removes only 20.5% of nodes vs. GND's 53.7%, and produces ~3.7× more fragments (8,705 vs. 2,371). GND is the right comparison here precisely because it, too, goes after a few structurally critical nodes rather than mass-harvesting cheap ones like FINDER.

On the real-world Openflights airline network, where a node is an airport and its cost is the total number of flights it serves, a 50% budget buys REND far more damage than GND: 19.6% of nodes removed vs. 37.2%, ~6.3× more fragments (1,646 vs. 261), a residual GCC less than half the size (22.2% vs. 51.5%), and an \(\Rrgcc\) of 27.6% vs. 82.1%. Since REND is optimized for \(\Rrgcc\) alone, winning simultaneously on node count, fragment count and GCC size is evidence that the corrected metric points at genuinely better strategies rather than at its own loophole.

The top-10 selections make the mechanism concrete. REND's ten airports cost about the same as GND's (0.27% vs. 0.23% of the budget), but removing them disconnects 75 additional airports from the global giant component, where GND's ten disconnect just one. Four of REND's picks are unremarkable-looking Alaskan airports — King Salmon, Juneau, Ralph Wien Memorial and Bethel — whose removal alone severs 31.3% (41/131) more airports from the GCC. None of them stands out by degree; what they share is the absence of backup connections, so their loss creates irreparable fragments. Degree-based centrality cannot see this, while REND finds the bridges that appear peripheral yet sustain the backbone connectivity of entire regions.

7Demystifying REND: the Boundary-Cut Heuristic

What did the black box learn? On the HI-II-14 protein network, over 90% of REND's top-K removals (\(K=1,10,20,30,50\)) are 2-core boundary nodes — formally, nodes that belong to the 2-core and have at least one direct neighbour in the 1-shell. They are neither expensive hubs nor trivial leaves, but low-cost bridges between the periphery and the core, and the pattern holds across most other real-world networks.

We distill this into Boundary-Cut: iteratively perform 2-core decomposition, remove boundary nodes in descending order of a cost-effectiveness ratio (associated 1-shell neighbours ÷ removal cost), and repeat until no boundary nodes remain. If the residual GCC is still above target, finish with HDA (or any other dismantling algorithm). Despite needing no training, its \(\Rrgcc\) averaged over nine real networks lands within ~2% of the deep-RL agent — 0.3440 vs. 0.3369 (degree-cost) and 0.3193 vs. 0.3150 (random-cost) — while substantially outperforming every other method. Being cheap to run and fully transparent, it is a deployable stand-in for REND.

8Conclusion

The standard GCC metric conflates structural fragmentation with trivial shrinkage, and that gap between evaluation and reality has long gone unnoticed. We close it in three steps: RGCC, a corrected metric that penalizes removal scale and rewards only genuine fragmentation; REND, a deep-RL agent that validates the metric by achieving cost-effective dismantling across diverse real networks; and Boundary-Cut, a transparent heuristic distilled from the agent's own policy that matches it without training.

The trajectory from metric correction to AI validation to heuristic distillation is the transferable part. As AI systems take on more real-world decisions, evaluation metrics must be designed with care, because an optimizer's power will find and exploit their hidden flaws. Used deliberately, that same power becomes a discovery tool — especially for optimization problems that are new or poorly understood.
